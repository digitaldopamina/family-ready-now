A first aid kit you bought at the airport and never opened is not a first aid kit — it's theater. This guide tells you exactly what to put in a kit that will actually function in the situations your family is likely to encounter.

## What a Home First Aid Kit Is (and Isn't)

A home first aid kit bridges the gap between an injury occurring and professional medical care arriving or becoming accessible. It handles:
- Minor wounds (cuts, scrapes, punctures)
- Burns (minor to moderate)
- Sprains and strains
- Allergic reactions (mild to moderate, plus anaphylaxis bridge to EMS)
- Nosebleeds
- Eye injuries
- Fractures and dislocations (immobilization pending professional care)

It is not a substitute for professional emergency care. When in doubt, call 911.

## The Complete Family First Aid Kit

Build this kit in a waterproof bag or hard-sided case. Everything visible and accessible — not crammed into a bag where you have to unpack it to find anything.

### Wound Care

**Sterile gauze pads:** Multiple sizes — 2×2, 3×3, and 4×4 inches. Used for wound packing, covering, and absorbing. This is the most-used item in a serious kit. Keep 10–20 of each size.

**Rolled gauze (Kerlix or similar):** For securing dressings, wrapping sprains, and wound packing. Keep 4–6 rolls.

**Wound closure strips (Steri-Strips or similar):** For lacerations that need closing but aren't severe enough for stitches, or as a bridge until stitches can be placed. Much easier to use than sutures for untrained responders.

**Butterfly closures:** Narrower than Steri-Strips, used for irregular wounds.

**Adhesive bandages (Band-Aids):** Assorted sizes including knuckle, fingertip, and large patches. The standard commercial kits usually have too many small ones and too few large patches — balance your stock.

**Hemostatic gauze (QuikClot or Combat Gauze):** Gauze impregnated with hemostatic agent (kaolin) that accelerates clotting. Dramatically reduces bleeding time for severe wounds. Squeeze into the wound, apply direct pressure for 3 minutes. Keep 1–2 packages. This is a significant upgrade from plain gauze for serious wounds.

**Non-stick dressings (Telfa):** For burns and abrasions — gauze sticks painfully to these wounds. Non-stick dressings change without reopening the wound.

**Wound wash/saline solution:** For cleaning wounds. Sterile saline is better than tap water and far better than hydrogen peroxide (which damages tissue). Wound wash cans are available at drugstores for $5–8.

**Medical adhesive tape:** Paper tape (less skin irritation), cloth tape (more secure). Both are useful.

### Bleeding Control

**Tourniquet (CAT or SOFTT-W):** A proper tourniquet, applied correctly and tightened appropriately, controls life-threatening limb bleeding that nothing else can. This is not the rubber strip from a cheap kit — it's a purpose-designed strap with a windlass that generates consistent pressure. The Combat Application Tourniquet (CAT) is used by the US military and EMS and costs ~$30. Keep one in your kit and know how to use it.

**Chest seals (HyFin or similar):** For penetrating chest wounds — prevents a pneumothorax (collapsed lung) from worsening. Vented versions are preferred. For families with hunters, rural outdoor activities, or any situation where penetrating trauma is possible.

**Pressure dressings (Israeli Bandage):** Combined dressing, bandage, and pressure mechanism in one unit. Excellent for head wounds, large area wounds.

### PPE

**Nitrile gloves:** Multiple sizes. Buy quality gloves that won't tear when you're trying to work quickly. Keep 4–6 pairs. Latex-free — latex allergies are common and unpredictable.

**CPR mask (pocket resuscitation mask):** Allows rescue breathing without direct mouth-to-mouth contact. Any trained rescuer should use one. Keep 2.

**Safety glasses or goggles:** Splash protection when dealing with significant bleeding or eye irrigation.

### Immobilization

**SAM Splint:** Flexible aluminum/foam splint that can be bent to any shape. Immobilizes wrists, ankles, fingers, forearms. One per kit; it handles multiple body parts.

**Elastic bandages (ACE bandage, 3" and 4"):** For compression wrapping of sprains, securing splints. Keep 2–4.

**Triangular bandage with safety pins:** Sling for arm injuries, general bandaging.

**Finger splints:** Small aluminum/foam splints for finger injuries. Keep 4–6 various sizes.

### Burn Treatment

**Burn gel (Water-Jel or similar):** Provides immediate cooling and pain relief. Far better than improvised home remedies (butter, toothpaste, ice). Apply after cool running water has lowered skin temperature.

**Non-adherent dressings:** Burns must not be covered with gauze that will stick. Non-stick (Telfa) or silicone-coated dressings.

**Plastic wrap (Saran Wrap):** An excellent temporary burn dressing — doesn't stick, maintains moisture, allows viewing of the wound. The American Burn Association recommends it as a temporary cover for transport to care.

### Medications

**Ibuprofen and acetaminophen (adult and children's):** For pain and fever. Both — they work through different mechanisms and can be alternated.

**Diphenhydramine (Benadryl):** Antihistamine for allergic reactions, hives, itching. Fast-acting.

**Aspirin (325mg tablets):** For suspected cardiac events in adults (not children). Chewing a standard aspirin while waiting for EMS can reduce heart attack damage.

**Antacid tablets:** For digestive discomfort, which can accompany stress responses.

**Electrolyte packets:** For dehydration from illness, heat, or exertion.

**EpiPen or Auvi-Q:** If anyone in your household has known severe allergies (nuts, bee stings, shellfish), an epinephrine auto-injector is non-negotiable. Keep two — one use each, and allergic reactions sometimes require a second dose before EMS arrives. These require a prescription. Keep them current — epinephrine degrades quickly after expiration.

### Assessment Tools

**Digital thermometer:** For fever monitoring.

**Blood pressure cuff (sphygmomanometer):** Useful but optional for most households. Essential if anyone in the home has a cardiac history or hypertension.

**Pulse oximeter:** Clips on a finger, measures blood oxygen saturation. Valuable for detecting respiratory illness severity (dangerous if O2 drops below 94%), COVID-19 monitoring, and altitude sickness assessment. ~$20–40.

**Penlight:** For pupil response assessment (especially after head injuries).

**Scissors (medical/trauma shears):** Cuts clothing to access wounds without moving the patient. Quality trauma shears ($10–20) cut through denim and seatbelts.

**Tweezers:** For splinter removal.

**Safety pins (assorted):** Closing slings, securing bandages.

### Documentation

**Printed emergency reference:** A laminated first aid reference card (American Red Cross publishes one). Instructions for CPR, choking, serious bleeding, burns.

**Local emergency numbers:** Poison Control: 1-800-222-1222 (US, 24/7). Your regional poison center. Local hospital address and phone.

**Medication allergy information:** Index card with every family member's name, allergies, blood type, and current medications. Update when anything changes.

## Kit Organization and Maintenance

**Container:** A waterproof bag or hard-sided container clearly labeled "FIRST AID." Everyone in the household should know where it is.

**Organization:** Zone by use — bleeding control in one pouch, medications in another, wound care in another. Clear labeling on each section.

**Maintenance schedule (every 6 months):**
- Replace expired medications
- Replace any opened sterile items (opened gauze loses sterility)
- Restock anything used
- Check tourniquet function
- Replace expired EpiPens

**Keep the kit with the kit:** Don't use the kit items for non-emergency purposes. Grabbing a bandage from the kit for a minor cut and not replacing it is how kits degrade.

## Skills Worth Having

Having the kit matters less than knowing how to use it. The Red Cross offers affordable, widely available classes:

- **First Aid/CPR/AED:** $20–80, 4–6 hours. Covers CPR, choking, bleeding, and basic first aid.
- **Stop the Bleed:** Free, 2 hours. Specifically covers severe bleeding control — tourniquet use, wound packing, pressure dressings. Find a class at bleedingcontrol.org.
- **Wilderness First Aid:** More comprehensive, designed for situations where EMS is delayed.
