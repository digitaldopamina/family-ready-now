Your car breaks down on the highway at night. A fender-bender in a rural area. A flat tire in the rain. A winter storm that leaves you stuck for hours. These are ordinary scenarios, not disasters — and the difference between a stressful inconvenience and a dangerous situation often comes down to what's in your trunk.

This guide covers what to actually carry, organized by priority, with specific product recommendations.

## Safety First: The Non-Negotiables

These items belong in every vehicle, always:

### Warning Devices
When you're stopped on the road, making yourself visible saves lives — including your own.

**Reflective triangles:** Place behind your vehicle to alert approaching traffic. Required by law in many states and standard in most countries. A set of 3 meets standard requirements. Keep them 50–100 feet behind your vehicle on a highway.

**Road flares or LED flares:** More visible than triangles, especially in rain. Traditional flares burn for 15–30 minutes (single use, check expiration). LED flares last years and are reusable — the Orion Safety Products LED Road Flares are durable and bright.

**Reflective safety vest:** Put it on before you get out of your car on a highway. Bright orange or yellow, with retroreflective strips. A $10 investment that could save your life.

### Light
**Flashlight or headlamp:** You need hands-free light to change a tire at night. A headlamp is better than a flashlight for this purpose. Keep fresh batteries or use a rechargeable model charged from your car's USB port. The Black Diamond Spot headlamp is reliable and affordable.

## Tier 2: Complete Kit (Primary Vehicle)

Add these to Tier 1 for comprehensive coverage:

### Tire Tools
**Tire inflator/air compressor:** Plugs into your 12V outlet and inflates a flat tire. Essential for the many flats caused by slow leaks rather than sudden blowouts. Viair and EPAuto make reliable models ($25–60).

**Tire sealant (Fix-a-Flat or Slime):** Temporarily seals small punctures. Buys you time to reach a shop. Check expiration dates every 2–3 years.

**Tire pressure gauge:** Keep one in the glove box. Proper tire pressure improves fuel economy, handling, and tire life — and many blowouts are caused by underinflated tires.

**Note on spare tires:** Know whether your car has a full-size spare, a "donut" spare (temporary use, 50 mph max, 50–70 miles max), or no spare at all (many newer cars use run-flat tires or only provide sealant kits). Check your owner's manual. If you have a spare, also know where your jack and lug wrench are located.

### Tools
**Jumper cables (traditional):** Even if you have a jump starter, traditional cables work between two vehicles and have no battery to die. Keep 16–20 foot heavy-gauge cables.

**Multi-tool:** Leatherman Wave or similar. Screwdrivers, pliers, knife — useful in dozens of situations.

**Duct tape and zip ties:** Solve temporary problems on the road. A torn mudflap, a loose hose, a broken trim piece — you'll find uses.

**Tow strap:** Rated 6,000+ lbs. For getting pulled out of a ditch or soft shoulder. Only use with vehicles equipped with proper tow hooks — never attach to bumpers.

**Folding shovel:** For digging out if stuck in snow, mud, or sand. Especially important in winter states.

### Sustenance (For Extended Wait Times)
**Water:** 1–2 liters per person. Water keeps indefinitely in sealed bottles but replace every year.

**Non-perishable snacks:** Protein bars, trail mix, jerky. Replace annually.

**Small cash:** A few $20 bills. Useful when card readers are down or if you need to pay for a tow quickly.

### Documentation
**Printed insurance card and registration:** Keep copies in the glove box — originals can stay home or in your wallet.

**Roadside assistance number:** Written on paper. AAA number, or your insurance company's roadside line.

**Local state police non-emergency number:** For highway incidents that don't require 911 but need a report.

**Paper map:** State road map or atlas. Download offline Google Maps or Maps.me for your region before long trips.

## Tier 3: Winter Kit (Cold Climate Vehicles)

If you live in a region with real winters, add:

**Ice scraper with long handle and brush:** You know you need this. The Subzero Heavy-Duty Extendable Snow Brush is good. Keep it accessible, not buried.

**Bag of sand or kitty litter:** Pour under tires for traction if stuck in snow or ice.

**Warm gloves and hat:** Stored in car November through March. Changing a tire in January without gloves is miserable and dangerous.

**Hand warmers:** Chemical warmers last 8–10 hours each. Keep 4–6 pairs.

**Sleeping bag or heavy blanket:** If you're stuck for hours, you need to stay warm. A sleeping bag rated to 20°F is small insurance against a serious situation.

**Windshield washer fluid (winter formula):** Rated to -30°F or lower for your climate. Running out during a snowstorm is dangerous.

**Booster cables + jumper:** Cold kills car batteries. Winter is the most common season for battery failures.

## Packing and Maintaining Your Kit

**Organization:** Use a medium-sized duffel bag or dedicated trunk organizer. Everything should be contained and not sliding around.

**Accessibility:** Heavy items in the trunk; frequently needed items (phone charger, flashlight) in the cab. Know exactly where your kit is — in an emergency, you don't want to be searching.

**Annual maintenance:**
- Check tire sealant expiration
- Check battery on jump starter and recharge if needed
- Restock used first aid supplies
- Replace expired food and water
- Check flare expiration dates
- Test your flashlight/headlamp batteries

**After use:** Replace anything you used as soon as possible. A kit with missing components is a kit that may fail when you need it.
