Rainwater harvesting ranges from a $50 plastic barrel at a downspout to a 10,000-gallon underground cistern supplying a home's entire non-potable water needs. The same underlying logic applies at every scale: collect what falls on your roof, store it properly, and use it.

This guide covers the full range, from beginner setup to serious residential systems.

## US Regulations: Know Before You Build

Rainwater harvesting regulations in the US have evolved significantly. Most states now permit or actively encourage it, but rules vary:

**States with explicit legal permission for residential collection:**
Colorado (since 2016 — previously one of the most restrictive), California, Texas, Oregon, Washington, and most other states. Texas actively incentivizes rainwater harvesting with a sales tax exemption on equipment.

**What most permits require:**
- System must be used on the property where collected
- Potable use requires treatment to NSF standards
- Large systems (500+ gallons) may need permits in some municipalities
- Systems connected to municipal water supply require backflow prevention

**Check:** Your state's DEQ (Department of Environmental Quality) or department of natural resources website, plus your local municipality. This takes 15 minutes and is worth doing before purchasing equipment.

## How Much Can You Collect?

The formula:
**Gallons collected = Roof area (sq ft) × Rainfall (inches) × 0.623 × Collection efficiency**

Collection efficiency is typically 80–90% (accounting for first-flush diversion and evaporation).

**Examples:**
- 1,500 sq ft roof, 20" annual rain, 85% efficiency = 15,900 gallons/year
- 2,000 sq ft roof, 40" annual rain, 85% efficiency = 42,300 gallons/year
- 1,000 sq ft roof, 10" annual rain, 85% efficiency = 5,300 gallons/year

**US average rainfall by region:**
- Pacific Northwest: 35–60 inches/year
- Southeast: 45–65 inches/year
- Midwest: 25–40 inches/year
- Southwest/arid West: 5–15 inches/year
- Northeast: 40–50 inches/year

Arid regions can still benefit from rainwater collection for garden use — they just need larger storage relative to collection area.

## System Components

### The Roof: Your Collection Surface

Your roof is your catchment area. Surface matters:

**Metal roofing** (corrugated, standing seam): Excellent. Minimal chemical leaching, easy to clean, durable. Best option for dedicated rainwater collection.

**Asphalt shingles:** Most common US roofing. Acceptable for irrigation and toilet flushing with first-flush diversion. Potential for some chemical leaching (polyaromatic hydrocarbons from asphalt). Not recommended for potable use without thorough treatment.

**Concrete/clay tiles:** Good. Minimal leaching.

**Avoid:** Roofs with lead flashing, roofs treated with biocides/moss killers, roofs near heavy industrial pollution.

### First-Flush Diverter

The first flush of rainwater carries the most contaminants — bird droppings, dust, pollen, debris — that accumulated on the roof since the last rain. A first-flush diverter automatically routes the first 10–15 gallons of runoff (per 1,000 sq ft of roof) away from your storage tank.

This is a non-negotiable component for any serious rainwater system. It significantly improves water quality with minimal cost ($20–80 DIY, $50–150 for commercial units).

**How it works:** A small side chamber fills with the contaminated first flush and slowly drains away. Once full, subsequent rainfall flows into the main storage.

### Gutters and Downspouts

Standard aluminum or copper gutters work. Keep them cleaned — debris in gutters becomes debris in your water. Install leaf guards to reduce debris entry. Copper gutters have some natural antimicrobial properties but cost significantly more.

Downspout connections should include a mesh screen to filter leaves and large debris before water enters the diverter or tank.

### Storage Options

**Above-ground rain barrels (50–500 gallons)**

The entry-level option. Simple, inexpensive, DIY-installable in an afternoon.

Basic setup: Connect a downspout diverter to a food-grade barrel with a spigot near the bottom, an overflow port higher up, and a screened lid. Ready-made units cost $50–150. DIY from a food-grade 55-gallon drum: $10–30 for the drum (often available used from car washes, food processors) + $20–30 in fittings.

**Limitations:** 50–500 gallons is useful for garden supplementation but not meaningful household backup. During a dry week, a small garden can use 500 gallons in 2–3 days.

**Linked barrel systems (500–2,000 gallons)**

Connect multiple barrels in series with a linking hose at the same level, sharing an overflow outlet. 10 × 55-gallon barrels = 550 gallons for $200–500. Significantly more capacity without major infrastructure.

**Above-ground tanks (500–10,000 gallons)**

Purpose-built polyethylene tanks (Poly-Mart, Tank-Depot, Rain Harvesting Supplies). More expensive but economical per gallon at scale. Must be opaque (light causes algae growth) and food-grade.

Typical prices: 500 gallons ~$300, 2,500 gallons ~$900, 5,000 gallons ~$1,500. Installation is straightforward for experienced DIYers.

**Underground cisterns (1,000–50,000+ gallons)**

Best for large-scale storage, cold climates (frost protection), and aesthetics. Much higher installation cost due to excavation. Used by serious off-grid homesteads and rural properties.

Polyethylene and fiberglass cisterns are most common. Concrete cisterns are more durable but require professional installation.

**Cost range:** $1,500–10,000+ installed for 1,000–5,000 gallons, depending on tank material and excavation complexity.

## Uses for Collected Rainwater

**Garden and landscape irrigation (no treatment needed):**
Most outdoor plants are indifferent to untreated rainwater — it's often better than municipal water because it's free of chlorine and fluoride. Vegetable gardens, fruit trees, flower beds, lawns.

**Outdoor cleaning (no treatment needed):**
Car washing, driveway cleaning, exterior window washing.

**Toilet flushing (minimal treatment — sediment filter):**
Toilets account for 24–30% of indoor household water use. Even a first-flush diverter + 50-micron screen provides adequate quality for toilet use. This is legal in most states for new construction and many retrofit situations (check local code).

**Laundry (sediment + carbon filter):**
Sand or cartridge filter removes particles; carbon filter removes chlorine-equivalent compounds. The resulting water is clean enough for laundry and extends washing machine life by reducing mineral buildup.

**Potable use (full treatment required):**
Multi-stage filtration: 10-micron sediment pre-filter → 1-micron sediment filter → carbon filter → UV sterilizer. Add a reverse osmosis stage for the highest quality. This level of treatment produces water that meets EPA drinking water standards.

## Sizing Your System

**For garden irrigation:** 
Calculate your peak garden water need (typically 1 inch per week = 0.6 gallons per sq ft per week for a vegetable garden). A 500 sq ft garden needs 300 gallons per week at peak. Size storage for 2–3 weeks between rains = 600–900 gallons minimum for drought resilience.

**For partial household use:**
A family of 4 uses approximately 100 gallons/day for all indoor use. Even replacing toilet flushing only (25–30 gallons/day) makes a significant dent. For this application, 500–1,000 gallons of storage with a good roof catchment is a practical starting point.

**For emergency backup:**
A 1-week supply for a family of 4 (non-potable use) = approximately 500 gallons. Add water purification capability for potable backup.

## Basic Maintenance

**Monthly:** Check and clear debris from gutters and first-flush diverter. Inspect tanks for algae growth (tank should be opaque).

**Twice yearly:** Clean tank interior (brush with diluted bleach solution, then rinse thoroughly). Inspect all fittings, screens, and connections. Test overflow function by running water through the system.

**Annually:** Clean downspout filters. Inspect roof for new contamination sources (new lead flashing, chemical treatments).
